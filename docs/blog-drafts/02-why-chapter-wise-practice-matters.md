**JEE maths rewards depth. Finishing a chapter properly, with practice and revision, beats rushing through the whole syllabus once.** The best way to get that depth is to work one chapter at a time: learn it, practise it, then revise it before you move on.

## Why depth beats speed in JEE maths

In JEE Main, the maths section has 25 questions and carries 100 of the 300 marks: 20 multiple-choice questions and 5 numerical-value questions. You get +4 for a correct answer and −1 for a wrong one, in both sections. (Details are in the [official JEE Main bulletin](https://jeemain.nta.nic.in/information-bulletin/).)

That marking scheme matters for how you study. A question you half understand can cost you marks twice: you lose time on it, and a wrong guess takes away a mark. A question you truly understand costs you less time and gives you the full four marks. So the goal of studying is not to *see* every topic once. It is to be able to *solve* questions in each topic on your own.

## The learn → practise → revise loop

Here is a simple loop to follow for each chapter:

1. **Learn.** Watch or read one small part of the chapter. Do not move on until you could explain the idea in your own words.
2. **Practise straight away.** Solve problems on that part while it is fresh. Start with easy ones, then move to harder ones.
3. **Mark your mistakes.** For every wrong answer, write down *why* it went wrong: a concept gap, a formula slip, a calculation error or a misread question. This list tells you what to fix.
4. **Revise.** Come back to the chapter after a few days and solve a mixed set again. If you can still do it, the chapter is yours.

This is why MathFlex puts practice right after every part. You finish a part, practise on exactly that topic, and only then does the next part open.

## Use past JEE questions, chapter by chapter

Previous-year questions (PYQs) show you how JEE actually asks about a topic. The best time to use them is *after* you have learnt the chapter, not before, and chapter by chapter rather than as one big mixed pile.

When you solve PYQs by chapter, you start to notice patterns: which sub-topics keep coming back, what kind of twist the exam likes, and which steps you tend to miss. You also get a clear picture of how ready that chapter is. Each MathFlex chapter comes with past JEE questions and practice problems after every part, with worked solutions to check against.

## Spot weak chapters early

If you practise chapter by chapter, weak spots show up quickly. Two signs to watch for:

- You keep making the same kind of mistake in a chapter, even after reading the solution.
- A new chapter feels much harder than it should, which usually means an earlier chapter was not solid.

Fix a weak chapter as soon as you notice it. Left alone, it gets worse, because JEE maths builds on itself.

## How the chapters connect

JEE maths is not a set of separate boxes. Chapters lean on each other, and knowing the links tells you what to revise when something feels hard.

### Functions → Limits → Calculus

[Functions](/chapter/functions) is the base of calculus. If you are unsure about domain, range or composition, then [Limits and Derivatives](/chapter/limits-and-derivatives) will feel confusing, and Continuity and Differentiability, Application of Derivatives and Integrals all depend on it. One limit you will use again and again is:

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$$

Later, integration brings the story full circle. Derivatives and integrals are linked by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, which you meet properly in [Definite Integrals](/chapter/definite-integrals):

$$\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx = f(b) - f(a)$$

### Trigonometry is everywhere

[Trigonometric Ratios and Identities](/chapter/trigonometric-ratios-and-identities) shows up in Complex Numbers, Coordinate Geometry, Vectors, 3D Geometry, Limits and Integrals. The identity you will use the most is:

$$\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$$

If your trigonometry is shaky, many other chapters will feel harder than they really are. It is worth getting it solid early.

## A simple weekly routine

Here is one routine you can adapt. It assumes you are studying one main chapter per week alongside school, and you should change the numbers to fit your timetable.

- **Day 1–2:** Learn the chapter in parts. After each part, solve its practice questions.
- **Day 3:** Finish any remaining parts and list the mistakes you made so far.
- **Day 4:** Solve past JEE questions for the chapter.
- **Day 5:** Redo the questions you got wrong, and write the key formulas on a one-page sheet.
- **Day 6:** Take a short mixed test on the chapter, plus a few questions from the previous chapter.
- **Day 7:** Rest, or catch up on whatever slipped.

Keeping one question from an older chapter in each week's practice stops old chapters from fading. If you want to see how this fits into a full year, read the [complete JEE maths plan](/blog/how-to-prepare-for-jee-complete-maths-plan).

## FAQs

### How many chapters should I study at the same time?

One main chapter at a time is a good rule. You can keep a second one for revision, but starting many chapters at once usually means finishing none properly.

### Should I solve PYQs before I finish a chapter?

Try a few easy ones to see the style, but save most of them for after you have learnt the chapter. Otherwise the questions mostly show you what you do not know yet.

### What if one chapter keeps going wrong?

Look at which earlier chapter it depends on and revise that first. For example, trouble in calculus often traces back to functions or trigonometry.

### Is chapter-wise study enough for JEE?

It builds the base. Closer to the exam you should add mixed tests so you can switch between topics under time pressure, which is how the real paper works.
