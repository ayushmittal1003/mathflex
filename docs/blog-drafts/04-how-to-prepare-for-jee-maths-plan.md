**To prepare for JEE maths, build your concepts chapter by chapter first, practise questions after every chapter, and add mock tests and revision as the exam gets closer.** A steady routine beats long, irregular study sessions. Here is a full plan you can adapt to your class and your timetable.

## When should you start?

- **Class 10:** There is no need for JEE-level questions yet. Use the time to make your algebra, factorisation, basic trigonometry and coordinate geometry solid. They are the base for everything later.
- **Class 11:** This is the best time to start. Class 11 chapters like Functions, Trigonometry, Quadratic Equations, Complex Numbers, Coordinate Geometry and Limits are the foundation of the whole JEE maths paper.
- **Class 12:** Keep going with the new chapters, and use your Class 11 knowledge actively. Calculus and vectors will lean on it heavily.
- **Droppers:** According to the JEE Main 2027 bulletin, you can appear if you passed Class XII in 2025 or 2026 (or are appearing in 2027). You have more time, so use it to fix weak chapters first, not to start a new random book.

## Know the paper you are preparing for

JEE Main 2027 Paper 1 is a 3-hour computer-based test with 75 questions and 300 marks. Maths has 25 questions: 20 multiple-choice and 5 numerical-value questions, worth 100 marks. You get +4 for a correct answer, −1 for a wrong one, and 0 for unanswered. Session 1 is in January 2027 and Session 2 in April 2027. Read [JEE Main vs JEE Advanced](/blog/jee-main-vs-jee-advanced) if you want the full picture.

## Step 1: Build concepts first

Do not start with hard problem books. For every chapter, first make sure you understand the definitions, the standard results and *why* a formula works. A chapter in which you only memorised formulas will fall apart the moment a question is twisted a little.

A good test: close the book and explain the chapter's main idea to a friend, or to yourself, in two minutes.

## Step 2: A sensible chapter order

You can change this, but this order works because each chapter prepares the next one.

### Class 11

1. [Functions](/chapter/functions), Sets and Relations
2. Trigonometric Ratios and Identities, then Trigonometric Equations
3. Quadratic Equations, then Complex Numbers
4. Sequence and Series
5. Straight Lines, Circle, then Parabola, Ellipse and Hyperbola
6. Permutations and Combinations, Binomial Theorem
7. [Limits and Derivatives](/chapter/limits-and-derivatives)
8. Statistics, Inequalities and the smaller chapters (Mathematical Reasoning, Principle of Mathematical Induction)

### Class 12

1. Inverse Trigonometric Functions, Matrices and Determinants
2. Continuity and Differentiability, then Application of Derivatives
3. Indefinite Integrals, Definite Integrals, Application of Integrals
4. Differential Equations
5. Vector Algebra and 3D Geometry
6. Probability, and the remaining chapters

The official JEE Main syllabus groups maths into 14 units (including Sets, Relations and Functions, Limit, Continuity and Differentiability, Integral Calculus, Coordinate Geometry and Trigonometry), so you can use it as a checklist. You can see every MathFlex chapter, for both classes, on the [chapters page](/chapters).

## Step 2b: Watch for links between chapters

Many chapters share ideas. For example, a complex number can be written using trigonometry, and one famous identity ties them together:

$$e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$$

Calculus is built on functions and limits. The derivative itself is a limit:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$

When you see these links, revising one chapter strengthens another. We explain more in [why chapter-wise practice matters](/blog/why-chapter-wise-practice-matters-for-jee).

## Step 3: A daily and weekly routine

Here is a routine you can scale up or down:

**Daily (on a normal school day)**
- 1 to 2 focused hours of maths. One hour of learning plus practice is better than three tired hours.
- Start with 10 minutes of revision of yesterday's work.
- End by writing down what you could not solve.

**Weekly**
- Pick one main chapter for the week.
- Spend the first part of the week learning it and practising straight after each part.
- Solve past JEE questions for that chapter.
- Keep one slot for redoing your mistakes and one for mixed revision.
- Keep one rest day. Rest is part of the plan.

On holidays and weekends you can add a longer block, for example for a mock test.

## Step 4: Practice and PYQs

Practise right after learning, and keep a *mistake notebook*. For every wrong answer, write the reason: a concept gap, a formula slip, a calculation error, or a misread question. Past JEE questions (PYQs) show you how the exam really asks about a topic, so solve them chapter by chapter once the chapter is learnt. MathFlex puts practice problems and past JEE questions after every part, with worked solutions.

## Step 5: Mock tests

Start mixed tests once you have covered a good part of the syllabus. The real JEE Main paper is 3 hours for three subjects, so as a rough guide you have about an hour per subject, though you can share the time as you like. Mock tests teach you things that chapter practice cannot:

- how to pick the questions you can solve and skip the rest, since a wrong answer costs a mark;
- how to manage your time across 25 maths questions;
- how to stay calm for three hours.

After each test, spend more time analysing it than you spent writing it.

## Step 6: Revision and formula sheets

Make a one-page formula sheet for every chapter as you finish it. Revise these sheets often, for example once a week, and redo the questions in your mistake notebook. A revision that only re-reads is weak. A revision that re-solves questions is strong.

## Step 7: Balance boards and JEE

Boards and JEE overlap a great deal in maths, so working on one helps the other. The JEE Main bulletin itself notes that having two sessions means a candidate who misses an exam for reasons such as the Board Examination does not have to wait a full year. Plan the heavy JEE tests away from your board exam dates, and use school work as extra practice on the same chapters.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Skipping the basics** and jumping straight to hard problems.
- **Only watching or reading**, without solving questions yourself.
- **Memorising formulas** without knowing where they come from.
- **Never revising**, so old chapters fade.
- **Ignoring weak chapters** because they feel uncomfortable.
- **Taking mock tests but not analysing them.**
- **Studying in bursts**, then stopping for weeks.

## Staying consistent

Consistency is mostly about making the plan small enough to follow on a bad day. Set a minimum, such as one hour and one chapter part, that you do no matter what. Track your streak, tell a friend or a parent what you are doing this week, and do not punish yourself if you miss a day. Just restart the next day.

## FAQs

### How many hours of maths should I study daily?

It depends on your class and school load, but a regular 1 to 2 hours of focused study, with more on holidays, is a good starting point. Regular beats long and irregular.

### Which chapters should I start with?

In Class 11, start with Functions and Trigonometry, then Quadratic Equations and Complex Numbers. They support many later chapters.

### When should I start mock tests?

Once you have covered a good part of the syllabus. Before that, chapter-wise practice teaches you more.
